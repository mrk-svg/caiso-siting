# Node Watch #1 — send

**Subject:** Would you check whether I got your substation wrong?

**Format:** plain text, individual sends, no BCC, no tracking pixel, no mailing-list software.
Personalise only the first line. Under 200 words — the ask has to survive a phone screen.

---

Hi [name],

[One specific line: where you know them from, or what they work on that made you pick them.]

I built a free tool that reads the CAISO public queue reports, the TPD allocation results, the
Local Capacity Technical Report and EIA-860, and publishes one page per interconnection node —
what's queued there, what share of filings survived each gate, what deliverability was requested
and refused, and what's already built. Every figure names the file it came from. There's no
score, no forecast and no dollar estimate, because the filings don't support any of those.

It has no users. Before it gets any, I want to know which numbers are wrong.

Would you open the page for a substation you know well and tell me the first thing that looks
off? [link] — one page, two minutes. Even "the POI grouping is wrong at X" is useful; I'd rather
find out from you than from someone relying on it.

Attached is the first Node Watch — seven things the data showed this month.

No list, nothing to subscribe to. Just this.

Robert

---

## Notes on the wording, so you don't soften it

- The ask is **"tell me what's wrong"**, not "let me know what you think". The second gets
  compliments; only the first gets corrections.
- "It has no users" stays in. It is true, it lowers the cost of criticising, and anyone who would
  hold it against you was never going to reply with a correction.
- Name the refusals — no score, no forecast, no dollar estimate — because that is what separates
  this from the three vendor emails they got this week.
- No list, no subscribe, no "let me know if you'd like to be added". This is a request for help,
  and turning it into a funnel is how it becomes ignorable.
- Do not attach a slide deck. One link, one note.
